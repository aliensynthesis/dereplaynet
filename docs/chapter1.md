# *~De Re PlayNET*<br>Chapter 1: A (Very) Brief History

PlayNET is the most influential online service that you've likely never heard of. It's also likely you've not heard of its technological successor, Quantum Link (aka Q-Link). Depending on your age, you may have heard of that service's direct successor, America Online. But maybe only in the stories your parents or grandparents tell of the times just before, and just after, the Internet was a thing.

All too often, obscurity is history's only reward for the visionary and the trailblazer.

The account of the PlayNET company presented here is intended to provide a basic context for the chapters that follow. It is knowingly incomplete because the extended story is largely undiscoverable. Perhaps someday soon, the actual participants of the saga may help correct this historical oversight and share their adventurers' tales in their own words, before it's too late. In the meantime, we must do with lore and legend.

## 1.1 The Legend

As the story goes, two research scientist coworkers from the GE Research Laboratory in Niskayuna, New York, decided to strike out on their own and create a new kind of computer game company. Instead of the traditional model in which customers would purchase games from a retail store, they envisioned an online experience in which customers would begin with an initial set of game disks and then, over time, download new games to add to their collection.

Such a system, named GameLine, already existed for users of Atari game consoles (minus the floppy disks). But the two partners, Howard Goldberg and David Panzl, wanted to take that concept further. Their games would be playable with other customers online at the same time. Not just that, the system would support real-time messaging so players could chat with each other while playing the games, or while gathered in dedicated chat rooms. It would also support electronic mail—a relatively new concept for general consumers at that time—allowing users to exchange messages with their PlayNET friends between online sessions.

They would also provide features for accessing other kinds of curated content, such as downloadable software libraries, special interest group events, and even online shopping for other kinds of merchandise. To top it off, the client software would be field-upgradable from the server, allowing the company to expand its feature set over time to keep pace with evolving client technologies.

But the key feature that would differentiate PlayNET from the other long-established online services offering similar capabilities, such as CompuServe, The Source, and DELPHI, would be the *quality* of the user experience. Instead of a plain text-based user interface like you might find on an office data terminal, PlayNET would sport a graphical user interface full of attractive colors, animated sprites, and non-scrolling screen transitions. Exactly the kind of interactive computing experience that game players were becoming accustomed to. It would be both online and *alive*.

It was an ambitious plan, to be sure, and they were well positioned to acquire the technical talent needed to pull it off. Already located practically on the doorstep of Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute, they had easy access to a pool of well-educated software engineers at all levels, as well as local entrepreneurial incubator programs and the networks of potential investors associated with them.

Having been exposed to software research programs at GE, the duo also brought with them knowledge of state-of-the-art data center technologies. They chose Stratus Computer as the vendor for their server hardware, known for its highly reliable transaction-processing capabilities, inherently fault-tolerant architecture, and extensive support for packet-switched network communications.

When it came to deciding on the client-side technology platform, there seemed to be only one bankable option at the time. Commodore Business Machines had already sold millions of its Commodore 64 computers, and they were among the least expensive options available when equipped with the color monitor, floppy disk drive, and modem required for the full PlayNET experience. The platform also had a respectable ecosystem of software development tools and excellent documentation resources. On balance, the Commodore line of 8-bit computers seemed to offer the perfect combination of capability and affordability for bringing the PlayNET experience into customers' homes.

By the second half of 1984 the stage was set for this capable team and its novel product to unleash a new and exciting experience upon the world of home computing. After roughly a year of systems development, the servers and back-office systems were in place, and the first client disk sets were ready for distribution.

The service went live in October 1984 after a modest advertising campaign, and by contemporary accounts it experienced respectable subscriber growth over the following months.

It's at that point that the story begins to fade.

If product reviews are any indication, the service seemed to enjoy an early period of success, with a rumored subscriber base of at least 5,000 customers at its peak. But within 18 months of going live, the company found itself in Chapter 11 bankruptcy proceedings before the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Northern District of New York.

Sometime during that period, the novel technology at the core of the client software was licensed to Control Video Corporation, which would secure the software's place in the post-PlayNET era and help beget another legend known as Quantum Link.

It appears that online operations continued through at least the second half of 1987, with some accounts suggesting it finally went dark sometime in early 1988. We could find no signs of life by 1989 or beyond.

## 1.2 The Facts

Disappointingly, we could find few verifiable details to substantiate this traditional account of events outlined above. We can verify a few things about the story's beginning, a few more about its middle, but practically nothing about its ending. For that reason we can only claim that the story of PlayNET is incompletely told here.

Below is a timeline of company milestones we were able to reconstruct from public-facing and directly attributable sources. The milestones are divided into startup, operational, and shutdown phases, whose boundaries are somewhat arbitrary but are reasonable we believe.

Much of the information here we haven't found chronicled in any other accounts. It's intended to serve primarily as a frame of reference to help place other findings into historical perspective. Think of it as a trail of factual guideposts, with the real stories waiting to be discovered somewhere in the spaces in between.

> **Intuitive Insights**: We're intrigued by the game named QUAD 64 and why the company went to the trouble of trademarking its name. The only other game they trademarked was SEA STRIKE, which would be familiar to anyone who has played the Milton Bradley/Hasbro tabletop game known as Battleship. Not only does it play the same way but the internal code name for the SEA STRIKE game module is "BTL". QUAD 64's internal code name is "TTT". We're reasonably sure that TTT is short for Tic-Tac-Toe, where in this case "quad" implies the use of a 4×4 grid instead of a 3×3 one, and "64" implies that it is extended into three dimensions (4×4×4). Pretty much as it's pictured in the advertisement at [RUN15]. Unfortunately, the project does not yet possess a usable image of the PlayNET GAMES 2 disk so we can't examine its contents to know for sure. Also note the two trademark symbols accompanying the names QUAD 64 and SEA STRIKE in the ad.
